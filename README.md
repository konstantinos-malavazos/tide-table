Four reefs are hidden on a grid. You get ten sonar pings to find them.

**Play it: <https://raw.githack.com/konstantinos-malavazos/tide-table/main/index.html>**

One HTML file, no dependencies, no build step.

## How it works

Tap a square to ping it. The number that comes back is how far away the nearest reef is.

That one number tells you two things:

- Every square **closer** than that number is empty. Guaranteed. They go grey.
- At least one reef sits at **exactly** that distance. Those squares go orange.

Ping again and you get a second ring. Squares that land on two rings at once go darker orange, and that is where the reefs are. A reading of 0 means you pinged straight onto one.

Ten pings, then mark your four squares and reveal.

The game teaches itself the first time you open it. Each step waits until you have actually done the thing.

## Is it a game?

The test is whether thinking about where to ping beats not thinking. Measured over 24 boards, at the full ten pings:

| how you play | reefs found |
|---|---|
| thinking about each ping | **3.96 of 4** |
| a sensible fixed pattern | 2.88 |
| pinging at random | 2.33 |

Thinking beats a fixed pattern by a whole reef, and good play narrows the board to exactly one possible answer on 19 of 24 boards. The budget bites too: four pings finds 2.0 reefs, ten finds 4.0.

That property is the whole point, and it is why this game replaced the one that was here before. See [DESIGN.md](DESIGN.md).

## Running the tests

```
node test.mjs
```

Eleven checks. Most of them are ordinary correctness, but one is the invariant the whole game rests on: **a square holding a reef is never ruled out.** If that ever broke, the deduction the board teaches you would be a lie.

The last four are balance properties. They assert that thinking beats a pattern, that a pattern is not enough on its own, that good play usually pins the board to one answer, and that spending fewer pings finds fewer reefs.

## What was here before

This repository used to hold a coastline-prediction game. You spent survey anchors on a hidden coast, an algorithm reconstructed it, and you were scored on the result. It is preserved in the git history up to commit `9b478d0`.

It was carefully built and it was not a game. Spending all twelve anchors scored 303 points; spending four scored 286. The one decision it offered barely mattered, and the best strategy was a fixed pattern you could learn in a sentence. The full accounting is in [DESIGN.md](DESIGN.md), including the measurements that killed it.
